University of Leicester Library: Making a 24-hour library easier for everyone to use

Service
David Wilson Library, University of Leicester: a 24-hour library with 1,500 study spaces
My role
Part-time UX researcher for the library, during my MSc in Human-Computer Interaction
Team
Three researchers from three departments
Timeline
2019 to 2020
Methods
Sampling plan, snowball recruitment, 30 interviews inside the library, on-site observation, card sorting
Five problem areas, from drinking water to wheelchair access, presented to the library director with recommendations.

The problem

The David Wilson Library at the University of Leicester is open 24 hours. It has 1,500 study spaces, 13 group study rooms, 350 computer places and around 38 km of shelving. The library team launched a "kindness audit" to understand what people experience in the building and what they need.

Research question: What makes the library hard to use, and which problems matter most to fix?

What I did and why

  • Chose who to talk to. The library serves students, teaching and non-teaching staff, library staff, locals and visitors. Entry data showed students and staff were by far the largest groups, so we focused on them.
  • Recruited through people we knew. Our team of three came from three different departments, and each of us recruited in our own department. We used snowball sampling: classmates referred friends, which helped us reach people quickly.
  • Interviewed people inside the library. We ran 30 interviews of about 30 minutes each, with consent and recordings. Holding them in the building mattered: several students walked us to the problem and showed us, which surfaced issues people would never think to mention in a meeting room.
  • Prioritized with card sorting. We listened back to every recording, wrote down each problem and need, and used card sorting to group them and decide which were most significant.

What I found, and what I recommended

1. Drinking water was hard to find, and hard to drink

Evidence: There were no signs for drinking water anywhere. The only taps were inside the toilets, and some weren't labelled. Some people told us they didn't notice the water for months, so they carried bottles every day. The water was also very cold, there were no kettles, and the café closed at 10 PM in a library open all night. During my own study hours, I saw someone heating water with a room heater.

Recommendation: Add signs and tap labels, and put a kettle on every floor.

2. Useful services were invisible

Evidence: The library lends laptops, but most people we interviewed didn't know. Headphones, mice and other equipment were available at the reception desk, with no sign saying so.

Recommendation: Label each service clearly where people will see it.

3. The movable shelves had a hidden safety risk

Evidence: Each shelving unit has two handles: one to move it and one to lock it. If someone walks between the shelves without locking them, another person can move the shelves from the other side while they're inside. The handles gave no clear signal of which did what.

Recommendation: Make the locking step obvious, with clear instructions and handles that show what they do. We also noted narrow gaps between shelves for wheelchairs, high shelves out of reach, and uneven lighting.

4. Wheelchair users met barriers all over the building

Evidence: The laptop checkout reader and the catalogue computers were above wheelchair reach. Basement display cases couldn't be read from a wheelchair. The lifts had no Braille. The accessible toilet needed a key that users didn't have, and the accessible entrance door had been broken for a month.

Recommendation: Lower key controls, add Braille, give access to the accessible toilet with a student ID card instead of a key, and fix the door so it opens with a standard card.

5. People got lost, and booking a room was slow

Evidence: Floor maps left out some rooms. The colour zones didn't say which subjects they held, and only the yellow zone had signs. Booking a study room or a computer was a long process, and there was no way to do it inside the library.

Recommendation: Add every room to the maps, label each zone with its subjects (for example, "Yellow zone: Mathematics, Computer Science"), add signs for every zone, and let people book rooms and computers at the self-service counter.

Outcome

We presented the findings and recommendations to the library director and team. They welcomed the work and planned to act on it.

What I learned

This was my first time doing research in the wild, on a physical space. Being in the building with people showed me problems no survey would have found, like a reader placed too high for a wheelchair user, or someone heating their water with a room heater at night.

It also showed me that the people a service most easily overlooks are often the ones who struggle most.

What I'd do differently: recruit beyond our own classmates, so we also hear from locals, visitors and wheelchair users directly, and follow up after the presentation to see which changes were made.
