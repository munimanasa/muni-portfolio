Skimli AI: Researching an AI video tool, from market analysis to clip editor

Five rounds of research took Skimli from emailed summaries to an AI clip editor tuned to 15 creator categories.

Project scope

Client
Skimli AI, an AI platform that turns long videos into short clips of key moments
Timeframe
Mar 2022 to Aug 2023
My role
UX researcher
Research methods
Interviews, survey, competitive analysis, personas, moderated usability testing, SUS, AI evaluation interviews, acceptance testing, AI training data labeling
Tools
Google Meet, Userbrain, Typeform, Clipchamp, Excel, Loom

Key insight

There is no single "key moment." A cooking channel and a comedy channel need different clips, and every creator wants control to fix what the AI picks.

That took Skimli from one-size-fits-all summaries to an AI clip editor tuned to 15 creator categories.

Project overview

Skimli uses AI to watch a long video and cut a short "skim" of its key moments. In 2022 it was an early-stage startup asking: which creators need this, what would make them trust an AI with their videos, and what does a "key moment" actually mean to them?

My role: UX researcher across the full cycle, from discovery to the AI evaluation. In total I ran 30+ interviews and usability tests and coordinated 50+ remote and hybrid sessions with creators around the world.

1Market analysis

Research question

Is there a market for an AI clipping tool, and where do existing tools fall short?

Methods

Desk research, competitive analysis, feature comparison

Who and what

Industry reports and 45 video tools, 6 compared in depth

  • ~50Mcreators worldwide (SignalFire, 2021)
  • ~8%of them are professionals
  • ~10Mvideo creators in the US (Wonder, 2021)
  • 45video tools I mapped
  • The market: a large and growing creator economy, with most creators working as amateurs on tight budgets. A tool had to save time to be worth paying for.
  • The workflow: creators stitch together several apps to record, edit, format and post. Every switch costs time.

Where Skimli stood out

  • Phone and tablet apps (none of the six had one)

Research impact

  • Positioning: showed where Skimli could win. None of the 6 tools compared offered a phone app or 30-second reels, and several didn't let creators edit their clips.
  • Priority: set time saved as the value creators would pay for, since most creators are amateurs on tight budgets.

The first product: Upload a long video, get a summary

Based on discovery, Skimli launched a simple try-it-out flow: paste a link or upload a long video, and the AI emails back a short summary.

  • First product: the needs found in discovery shaped Skimli's first release, a simple upload-a-video, get-a-summary flow.

3Website testing: Creators wanted an editor

Research question

Can a first-time creator get a summary on their own, and is a summary what they need?

Methods

Moderated remote usability testing, task ratings, SUS questionnaire, interviews

Who and what

Creators screened against a short survey, October 2022

  • 88average System Usability Scale (SUS) score
  • 68commonly cited average SUS score
  • 4.7average ease rating out of 5 for finishing tasks

The score was excellent. The conversations told a different story.

"It is quite impressive, but I normally do not make summary videos. Instead I create small clips to share on Twitter and YouTube Shorts."

Usability study participant

Creators wanted to personalize what the AI made

Creators didn't only want an AI summary of their video. They wanted the freedom to personalize on top of what the AI created: edit the clips, add thumbnails and share them to each platform in their own style. Summaries also didn't work for short videos: when a creator uploaded a video under about 4 minutes, the AI summary came out only a few seconds long, too short to post or use.

The email wait risked losing creators

Creators had to leave the website, go to their email and wait. For a 1 hour 16 minute video, the email said the clips could take over 9 hours. After that long, creators might forget to come back and open their clips at all.

Research impact

  • A new product: we decided to build a web app where creators create a profile, upload their videos, watch the progress, and edit, share and download their clips, all in one place.

Why the conversations led: these were qualitative sessions, meant to find problems and needs, not to measure them. The SUS score confirmed the site was easy to use; what creators said drove the decision.

4Editor testing: Finding and fixing issues

Research question

Can creators edit, save and share clips without help, and does the editor meet every requirement?

Methods

Usability sessions, acceptance testing, bug reports with video walkthroughs, retesting

Who and what

Creators and the product team, December 2022

Creators watched a clip, edited and saved it, downloaded it, renamed it and cancelled an edit. I also tested every acceptance criterion, logged each gap with the expected behaviour, and retested the fixes.

Research impact

  • Safer release: I logged 7 editor issues with video walkthroughs, and 3 were resolved (clip order, timeline zoom and jumping timestamps).
  • Better editing: my finding that creators couldn't type exact times led to typed Start and Length fields in the improved editor.

5AI evaluation: Different creators, different moments

Research question

Does the AI pick the moments a creator would pick, and does that differ by type of creator?

Methods

Comparative evaluation (AI skim versus a manual Clipchamp edit), interviews, follow-up survey, video labeling, model training, features document

Who and what

Creators reviewing skims of their own videos; then creators in 15 content categories

What counts as a key moment depends on the creator

One model for everyone missed the mark. So the research expanded to creators in 15 content categories. Interviews explored what makes a moment worth clipping in each niche, and their needs were fed into training the AI.

Why categories need different clips

The clearest contrast was between cooking and comedy creators. They use short videos for different jobs, so the right moment is different.

The pattern: for some categories a short video is a hook for the long one. For others it is the content itself. The AI had to learn which job each creator needs before it could pick the right moment.

From research to training data

The category needs set what the AI should learn. I then turned them into training data: I collected long videos with the short clips each channel had actually posted, labeled their key moments, trained a model for each category, and wrote the features document describing what the AI should look for.

  • 151long videos collected, about 145 hours
  • 116videos I labeled for key moments
  • 13category models I trained, deployed to development

Research impact

  • Smarter AI: my features document turned the needs of creators in 15 categories into what the AI learns, moving it from one generic cut to moments that fit each niche.
  • Measurable result: the research contributed to a 20% improvement in AI summary efficiency and supported Skimli's seed funding round.
  • Training at scale: I built a training set of 151 long videos across 15 categories, labeled 116 of them, and trained models for 13 categories that were deployed to development.
  • A new way to judge the AI: comparing the AI's skim with a manual edit of the creator's own video gave the team a clear benchmark for clip quality.

Outcome

  • Clear directionPersonas and a 45-tool competitive map, showing Skimli as the only one in the comparison with a phone app and 30-second reels.
  • Product shiftFrom emailed summaries to a clip editor creators can edit, share and export, driven by what creators said in testing.
  • Safer releaseEvery editor requirement tested, with 3 of the 7 issues I logged resolved.
  • Smarter AIMy research contributed to a 20% improvement in AI summary efficiency, and needs from 15 creator categories fed into training, so the AI picks teasers for cooking creators and complete jokes for comedy creators.

Reflection

Industry takeaways

AI products are judged against what the user would have done themselves. Testing with each creator's own video, and comparing it with a manual edit, gave feedback we could act on.

Personal takeaways

A strong usability score is not the same as the right product. Every participant finished every task, yet the most important finding was that they wanted something different: clips they could personalize, not summaries.

What I'd do differently

Test with more people, and set up metrics before each release, such as clips edited, clips shared and returning creators, so I could measure the impact of each change.
